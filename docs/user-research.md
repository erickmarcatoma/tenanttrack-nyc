## Interview guide (30 min)

### Before the interview

- Confirm the participant is an NYC tenant who has dealt with a repair problem. Don't collect their name, address, apartment number, immigration status, or case details.
- Ask which language they prefer and use an interpreter only if one is available and the participant agrees.
- Use a prepared demo building or let the participant observe without entering an address. Don't record the session by default.
- If considering a recording or using any quote, get separate permission first. Keep notes anonymous and share only de-identified themes.

### Opening script (2 min)

> Thanks for speaking with me. I'm learning how tenants find information about housing repairs and testing an early product idea. This is not legal advice or a service intake, and taking part will not affect services or benefits. You can skip any question or stop at any time. Please avoid sharing names, exact addresses, apartment numbers, or details that could identify you or someone else. I will take anonymous notes; I won't record unless we discuss that separately and you agree. Is it okay to continue?

### Questions (about 25 min)

1. Tell me generally about a recent repair problem you experienced. Please don't name people or give an exact address.
2. What did you do first? What information or help did you look for?
3. Did you contact 311, HPD, a tenant group, or another organization? What was that experience like? (Skip if you prefer.)
4. How did you figure out what needed to be repaired or when it should be fixed?
5. What was the most difficult or confusing part?
6. Which language would you prefer for this information? Are there words that are hard to understand?
7. **Prototype:** Using the prepared demo (or just looking at it), what do you think this page is telling you? What would you do next? What is unclear or missing?
8. Would you use a letter drafted by an app? What would you need to know before trusting or sending it?
9. What else should we have asked?

### Close (3 min)

Thank the participant, ask whether they want a summary of the project when available, and remind them how to contact you with questions. Do not promise legal follow-up or outcomes.

## Synthetic practice interviews (fictional; not research evidence)

The following scenarios and responses are invented to rehearse the interview guide and explore possible usability questions. They are not real tenants, quotes, observations, or findings. Do not include them in the interview log, report them as research, or use them to claim what tenants generally think. Replace this section with real, consented, de-identified notes as interviews take place.

### Practice 1: Finding out whether a repair was reported

**Fictional scenario:** An English-speaking tenant in the Bronx has had a leak under a kitchen sink. They told the super, but don't know whether a 311 complaint exists.

- **What they tried:** They asked the super and searched online, but were unsure what details to enter.
- **Possible prototype reaction:** They can identify an open complaint, but ask whether the page proves that the leak is still happening.
- **Practice prompt for the interviewer:** Ask what they would expect a complaint status to mean; don't assume that a city record is complete or current.

### Practice 2: Understanding an overdue notice

**Fictional scenario:** A Spanish-preferring tenant in Brooklyn sees peeling paint and a window that does not close properly. They want to understand which issue needs attention first.

- **What they tried:** They asked a family member to interpret an online page and found the official wording difficult.
- **Possible prototype reaction:** They understand a short Spanish explanation, but think “overdue” means the city will automatically make the landlord repair it.
- **Practice prompt for the interviewer:** Check what action the status implies to them, and whether the app clearly distinguishes a data-based deadline from enforcement or a guarantee.

### Practice 3: Trusting building data

**Fictional scenario:** An English-speaking tenant in Queens has recurring heat problems and is comparing what they see in an app with what neighbors have reported.

- **What they tried:** They called 311 once but are unsure whether a complaint was closed or the underlying condition fixed.
- **Possible prototype reaction:** They ask where the data came from and when it was last updated.
- **Practice prompt for the interviewer:** Ask what source and freshness information would help them judge the page; don't suggest that public records reflect every tenant's experience.

### Practice 4: Using the letter draft

**Fictional scenario:** A Spanish-preferring tenant in Manhattan wants to ask a landlord to repair a persistent bathroom problem but is concerned about sending inaccurate or overly forceful wording.

- **What they tried:** They have sent text messages before and want a clear, respectful written record.
- **Possible prototype reaction:** They like a draft in Spanish but want to edit it, see an English version, and understand that the app does not send it.
- **Practice prompt for the interviewer:** Ask what they would change and what would make the draft feel safe to use; don't frame sending a letter as required or risk-free.

### Practice 5: Using the app on a phone

**Fictional scenario:** An English-speaking tenant in Staten Island uses a low-cost phone and has intermittent mobile data. Their building has multiple maintenance issues.

- **What they tried:** They use search on a phone but often abandon long pages and don't know which issue to discuss first.
- **Possible prototype reaction:** They want the main status and next-step information near the top, readable text, and a way to revisit a report.
- **Practice prompt for the interviewer:** Observe where they pause or lose their place in the demo; don't treat this invented reaction as evidence of actual accessibility.
